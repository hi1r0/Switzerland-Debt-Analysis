## Abstract

This paper reviews Switzerland’s public debt trajectory using selected observations from 1990 to 2023. The evidence shows that nominal debt increased over the sample period, but debt sustainability remained strong because GDP expanded more quickly after the mid-2000s and the debt-to-GDP ratio moved back toward 20%.

Debt per capita increased from CHF 6,776 in 1990 to CHF 17,977 in 2023, while GDP per capita grew more sharply, supporting the view that Switzerland maintained fiscal stability despite major global shocks.

### Study Scope

The study uses selected yearly observations for 1990, 1995, 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, 2020, and 2023. The core indicators are national debt, population, GDP, debt-to-GDP ratio, debt per capita, and GDP per capita. Currency references are reported in Swiss francs (CHF). The source document also records an exchange rate of 1 CHF = 111.15 INR on 21 October 2025.

# 1. Introduction

National debt represents the cumulative amount a government owes to domestic and foreign creditors. Looking at debt on a per-capita basis provides a more intuitive measure of the burden associated with public borrowing because it links the debt stock to the size of the population.

Switzerland offers a useful case study because it combines a highly developed economy, strong institutions, and a reputation for conservative fiscal management.

This paper studies Switzerland’s debt position from 1990 to 2023 using selected observations on national debt, population, GDP, and the debt-to-GDP ratio. The objective is not only to describe how debt changed over time, but also to interpret whether those changes imply fiscal stress or fiscal sustainability.

---

# 2. Country Overview

Switzerland has one of the world’s most advanced and stable economies. Its production structure is led by services, especially financial services, while high-value manufacturing remains central through pharmaceuticals, chemicals, precision instruments, and luxury goods.

The country benefits from political stability, low unemployment, a skilled workforce, and a currency that is often treated as a safe-haven asset in periods of global uncertainty.

By 2023, Switzerland’s population reached approximately 8.89 million people. Because the economy remained productive and outward-oriented across the period studied here, changes in public debt need to be interpreted alongside output growth rather than in isolation.

# 4. Calculation of Debt per Capita

For internal consistency, debt per capita is computed as:

```math
Debt\ per\ capita_t = \frac{National\ debt_t}{Population_t}
```

For comparison with broader living-standard capacity, GDP per capita is also calculated as:

```math
GDP\ per\ capita_t = \frac{GDP_t}{Population_t}
```

The derived values in Table 2 show that debt per capita increased from CHF 6,776 in 1990 to CHF 17,977 in 2023. However, GDP per capita rose more strongly over the same period, reinforcing the view that Switzerland’s debt remained manageable relative to national income.

# 5. Analysis

Because the observations are sampled at multi-year intervals rather than annually, each growth rate in Table 3 compares an observation with the preceding observation.

Debt per capita grew rapidly between 1990 and 1995, continued rising through 2005, and then declined markedly between 2005 and 2010. Population growth was steadier and remained positive in every interval.

This comparison matters because it separates two forces that influence debt burden. When population rises steadily while debt grows more slowly, the per-capita burden can stabilize or even fall. That pattern is visible after 2005, when debt per capita either declined or grew only modestly while GDP continued to rise.

# 6. Discussion

Several points emerge from the data.

First, nominal debt clearly increased over time, but the debt burden did not deteriorate in the same proportion because GDP grew substantially, especially after 2005.

Second, the debt-to-GDP ratio returned to a lower and more stable range after its mid-2000s peak, which is consistent with Switzerland’s reputation for fiscal discipline.

Third, population growth helped spread the debt burden across a larger number of residents. That does not eliminate debt, but it reduces the pace at which debt per capita rises when the population expands steadily.

Finally, the fall in debt per capita between 2005 and 2010 suggests that Switzerland navigated the global financial crisis without allowing the public debt burden to become structurally unstable.

---

# 7. Interpretation

In practical terms, the 2023 estimate implies a debt burden of about CHF 17,977 per resident. On its own, that number may appear large.

In context, however, it sits alongside a GDP per capita of roughly CHF 99,565 and a debt-to-GDP ratio near 20%. These ratios indicate that Switzerland had strong repayment capacity and that public borrowing remained within sustainable bounds.

The broader interpretation is that fiscal sustainability depends less on the absolute size of debt and more on how debt compares with national income, institutional credibility, and growth capacity. Switzerland performs well on each of these dimensions.

---

# 8. Conclusion

Switzerland demonstrates how a growing economy and disciplined public finance framework can maintain debt stability over time.

Between 1990 and 2023, the country experienced higher nominal debt, but that increase was offset by strong GDP growth, steady population growth, and an improvement in the debt-to-GDP ratio after 2005.

The resulting debt per-capita profile supports the conclusion that Switzerland remains a strong example of sustainable sovereign debt management.
